# El Niño Seed Supply Risks: Is the Industry Prepared?

A powerful climate signal in the Pacific can’t predict the outcome of a single field. It can, however, expose where seed production, quality, and supply plans are carrying more risk than anyone realized. El Niño begins with warm water in the tropical Pacific. But for the U.S. seed industry, the consequences can surface thousands of miles away — in a production field, a conditioning schedule, an inventory decision, or a contract that suddenly becomes much harder to fill. That doesn’t mean one climate index can forecast the fate of a crop. It can’t tell a seed producer whether a particular field will catch a critical rain, face a damaging heat wave, or move through harvest without delay. What it can do is shift the odds across entire production regions and expose weaknesses in plans built around normal conditions. Dr. Mohammad Azhar Ehsan, a research scientist at Columbia University’s Center for Climate Systems Research, describes the developing 2026 El Niño as unusually strong and still gaining strength. The event had moved from neutral conditions in winter 2025-2026 to borderline El Niño in spring, then through weak and moderate stages to very strong by summer. “On a seasonal scale, that’s a rapid increase,” Ehsan says. Forecast models point to further strengthening and persistence into spring 2027. For seed companies, producers and breeders, the immediate question isn’t whether El Niño guarantees a specific outcome. It’s whether the industry has enough flexibility if temperature and precipitation patterns move outside the assumptions behind production and supply plans. More Than A Line On An Ocean Map El Niño is the warm phase of the El Niño-Southern Oscillation, or ENSO, a recurring change in tropical Pacific Ocean temperatures and atmospheric circulation. Scientists monitor sea-surface temperatures, but Ehsan says conditions below the surface are equally important. A large reservoir of warm water can sustain the signal at the surface for months. Dr. Mohammad Azhar Ehsan, a research scientist at Columbia University’s Center for Climate Systems Research “The subsurface temperature is actually the fuel for the signal for the next several months,” he says. That warmer water changes winds and the path of the jet stream, which can redistribute temperature and precipitation far beyond the Pacific. In the seasonal outlook Ehsan says much of the northern tier of the United States — particularly the Pacific Northwest — leans warmer than normal for winter. The precipitation outlook favored wetter conditions across parts of the southern United States and drier conditions farther north. Those broad patterns overlap several important seed-production regions, but they shouldn’t be read as field-level predictions. A seasonal probability above 50% doesn’t mean temperatures will be 50% warmer. It means the odds favor the above-normal category over the alternatives. Cold days can still occur in a warm winter, just as an individual storm can land in a season that ends drier than normal. “El Niño doesn’t mean every day will be warmer,” Ehsan says. “What it does shift is the odds towards a warmer seasonal temperature regime in the Pacific Northwest.” Seed Production Concentrates Risk Commodity production can often absorb some regional variability because volume is spread across millions of acres. Seed production is a narrower system. A specific hybrid, inbred, variety, or generation may be produced on limited acres in a limited number of locations, often under tight quality standards and delivery timelines. That concentration turns a regional climate disruption into a potential supply problem. Excess moisture can interfere with planting, pollination, disease management, and harvest. Heat or drought can reduce seed set, alter maturity, or create uneven lots. Warm harvest conditions can complicate drying and conditioning. The risk isn’t simply fewer bushels. It’s fewer bushels that meet germination, purity, vigor, moisture, and phytosanitary requirements. The first useful question for a seed company is therefore geographic: where are critical products, parent lines, and multiplication acres concentrated? The second is operational: what happens if one of those regions loses yield or quality at the same time another faces harvest or transportation delays? El Niño doesn’t answer those questions. It gives the industry a reason to ask them early, while backup production, inventory transfers, and customer communication are still possible. Quality Risk Doesn’t End at Harvest A warmer seasonal background can follow seed from the field into drying, conditioning, and storage. Seed arriving warmer or wetter than expected may require more drying capacity and energy. A delayed or compressed harvest can crowd receiving systems and leave less time to separate lots, complete quality testing, and make disposition decisions. The effects will differ by crop and region. For some seed, the concern may be mechanical damage or reduced vigor after a difficult harvest. For others, it may be disease development, dormancy, moisture migration, or the challenge of maintaining stable storage conditions through a warmer winter. These aren’t reasons to assume quality will decline. They’re reasons to check whether dryer capacity, storage controls, sampling plans, and testing timelines can handle a less orderly season. A contingency that exists only after an affected lot arrives at the plant isn’t much of a contingency. The Market Impact May Start Somewhere Else El Niño’s reach also makes it a market story. Ehsan’s global outlook shows strong precipitation signals in parts of South America, southern Africa, Australia, and the United States. Weather in those regions can influence seed production, commodity prices, acreage decisions, and demand long before a direct U.S. supply shortage appears. The interaction with other climate patterns adds another layer. Ehsan points to the Indian Ocean Dipole, which can reinforce hot and dry conditions in parts of Australia when it develops alongside El Niño. That pattern hasn’t fully developed, making it a factor to watch rather than a settled outcome. For an industry operating across hemispheres, trouble in one production region can affect counter-season multiplication, parent seed availability, and the ability to recover from a shortfall in another. It can also change the crop economics customers face. A shift in commodity prices, insurance expectations, or water availability can change planted acres — and seed demand — even when the original weather event occurred far from the sales territory. Planning Without Pretending to Know Seasonal climate forecasts can be frustrating because they stop short of the certainty businesses want. They can’t identify the exact county, week, or crop stage where trouble will occur. That’s not a reason to ignore them. It’s a reason to use them for scenario planning rather than precise prediction. Seed businesses can begin by identifying the products with the least production redundancy, the regions most exposed to the forecast pattern, and the quality bottlenecks that would be hardest to solve during harvest. They can review carryover inventory, alternative production locations, testing capacity, freight options, and the dates by which supply decisions must be made. The same review should include customers. If winter precipitation, snowpack, or reservoir levels begin to point toward water constraints, demand assumptions may need to change before planting. If warmer conditions accelerate fieldwork or crop development in another region, delivery and treatment schedules may move with them. The goal isn’t to rebuild a production plan every time a forecast changes. It’s to establish decision points. What will the company monitor? What level of soil moisture, snowpack, reservoir storage, or regional crop stress would trigger action? Which actions become more expensive — or impossible — if they wait another month? An Early Warning, Not A Verdict Ehsan emphasizes that the event isn’t yet at the point in its normal cycle when ENSO typically peaks. That leaves considerable uncertainty around the eventual strength and regional outcome. It also leaves time. Seed companies can watch the climate signal develop alongside local forecasts and field conditions, adjusting only when the evidence supports it. That’s the real value of the outlook, Ehsan says. El Niño isn’t a verdict on the 2027 seed supply, and it shouldn’t be used to manufacture one. It’s an early warning that the distribution of risk may be changing across production regions, storage systems, and markets. For a sector that depends on producing the right seed, in the right place, at the right quality, and at the right time, a shift in the odds isn’t background noise. It’s information worth putting into the plan.
