# Vylor Is Open for Business: What Changes for Europe’s Seed Sector

Vylor, the new standalone advanced seed and genetics company, brings established brands, germplasm and global R&D scale. The more interesting story is how the company intends to use them. Exactly one year after Corteva announced plans to divide its seed and crop protection businesses, Vylor officially came into being on 1 October 2026. For Europe’s seed sector, that creates an unusual kind of new company. The name is new. Much of the underlying business is not. Pioneer® remains the flagship farmer-facing brand, established breeding programs continue, and Vylor starts life carrying over a large global research, production and commercial footprint. What changes is the structure around those assets. Jean-Philippe Riffat, who leads Vylor across EMEA and APAC, describes the opportunity as creating “a global pureplay agricultural genetics platform” able to take existing scientific leadership into new areas. The proposition that Vylor will be launching to market over the coming weeks, including at Euroseeds, is that the core business remains seed, but the ambition is broader: new crops, technologies, uses and partnerships. Across conversations with Vylor’s incoming EMEA-APAC leadership, three messages come through most clearly for the seed sector: Vylor is ready to operate independently, innovation will be central to its growth, and collaboration with other seed companies will play a much larger role in how it goes to market. “We’re open for business as a genetics, technology and licensing partner,” says Riffat. The Familiar Brands Remain Loïc Boucher, Vylor’s EMEA and APAC Marketing and Commercial Effectiveness Leader, says Pioneer, Brevant® and other established commercial brands will remain grower-facing. Vylor has a different role: it is the corporate and partner-facing identity behind the genetics, technology and licensing business. “Vylor will establish its own identity by providing competitive genetics, delivering reliability and helping its customers, the partners, to grow,” Boucher says. Its relevance to the seed trade will therefore depend less on putting a new corporate logo on bags of seed and more on what sits behind the name: genetics, licensing, technology, technical support and different routes to market. Vylor is also genuinely separate from Corteva. André Negreiros, Vylor’s Southern Europe Commercial Unit Leader, says that was one of the most common points of confusion he encountered ahead of the split: whether Vylor and Corteva would separate while somehow remaining parts of the same company. “This is definitely not the case. We’re two independent companies,” Negreiros says. “There will be tons of agreements between the two companies, but we are completely separate. The leadership is different and, over time, potentially the investors will be different. We are two different companies now with two different goals.” Where Vylor Sees Room to Grow Riffat sees three key routes for Vylor to expand its genetics business. Taken together, those three opportunities show an innovation strategy that extends beyond simply developing a hybrid that outperforms the one before it. The first route is to get more from crops where Vylor already has substantial breeding depth. Gene editing, not surprisingly, will be central to that pillar. “Through gene editing, we’re going to put more solutions in the plant for farmers,” he says, describing gene editing as “a core of our future as a company.” Riffat points to resilience, including the ability to maintain performance under drought and other environmental pressures, as an important target for the genetics pipeline. The second route is to take Vylor’s breeding capabilities further into additional crops. Hybrid wheat is the clearest example. “We are planning to launch hybrid wheat in 2027. We have a proprietary hybridization system in wheat,” Riffat says. “That’s an expansion of our genetic platform to a crop where we have not been present.” The third route is different again: use crops Vylor already understands to address emerging markets and production systems. For example, Vylor is working on a short-cycle sunflower that could fit between established crops such as wheat and barley, producing an additional harvest without displacing the primary rotation. The sunflower could supply biofuel production, with the meal co-product contributing protein for animal feed. The regulatory environment will inevitably influence how quickly some of those technologies reach the market. Teresa Babuscio, Vylor’s Head of Government and Industry Affairs for EMEA and APAC, sees Europe at a different point than it was even a few years ago. “Europe has learned a lesson, and it does not want to miss another chance,” she says, pointing to geopolitical pressure, climate change and the opportunities European agriculture missed around earlier biotechnology. She sees the EU’s new NGT framework as evidence of a more pragmatic approach, with the bigger test now being how that framework is implemented in practice. Open For Business Beyond Its Own Brands Vylor’s innovation strategy is only part of the story. The other key for Europe’s seed sector is where genetics and technology will be available outside Vylor’s own commercial brands. Vylor is “open for business” with the wider seed sector, Riffat says. That includes licensing genetics and technology, working with seed companies that have stronger access to particular crops or markets, and looking for partners that can take Vylor innovation further than Vylor’s own commercial organisation could alone. Boucher says Vylor is consolidating teams around licensing and building a dedicated identity for that part of the business: Vylor One. The model he describes goes beyond supplying a hybrid or variety. Under the model, Vylor will provide genetics, pipeline, product knowledge and support, while the partner will leverage those assets to build its own portfolio under its own brand and according to its own market strategy. “We would provide the background support in order for all partners to build their own approach, touching their own cus­tomers,” Boucher says. He describes licensing as “a very strong component” of Vylor’s strategy going forward. Boucher is explicit that Vylor does not want a licensing relationship built around dependency. Partners, he says, should retain control over their market access, grower relationships, brands and agronomic expertise. Boucher puts it simply: “Vylor won’t be visible for the farmers, and probably that’s the strength there.” A Different Test of the New Company Vylor’s scale, brands and breeding programs are already estab­lished. The questions now are more behavioural. Does Vylor become easier for another seed company to access? Does Vylor One materially expand the licensing pipe­line? Does its genetics platform move successfully into additional crops and markets? Do gene-editing tools translate into commer­cially relevant products in Europe? Can projects such as interme­diate sunflower connect breeding innovation to emerging value chains? And does concentrating the organization entirely around seed and genetics make it faster? Boucher believes it should. He describes a more stream­lined organization where expertise from the top to the bottom is focused on the seed business. “Agility, speed for supporting our partners. That is the key message,” he says. Vylor already has the genetics, people, brands and infra­structure to be consequential. What the European seed sector can begin judging now is whether independence changes how those assets reach the market, and how many other seed com­panies can build with them.
