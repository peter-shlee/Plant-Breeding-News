# The Seed Sector Has a Connection Problem, Not an Innovation Problem

This week’s stories all point to the same conclusion: innovation alone isn’t enough. The future belongs to the people who can make connections between science, infrastructure and farmers. If you frequent the LinkedIn platform, you’ll know that connections matter. This week’s stories illustrate that to a tee. In fact, our very future might depend on the ability to connect. Marc Zienkiewicz is senior editor for Seed World Canada One story explores breeding corn that resists aflatoxin while delivering better nutrition, climate resilience and competitive yields. Another asks whether farmers should lead Canada’s wheat breeding system. A third examines whether seed retail needs its own “Amazon moment” by simplifying how seed moves from companies to farmers. Add concerns about the plant breeding workforce and a new agreement to preserve Saskatchewan’s Indian Head and Scott research farms, and a common theme emerges. The future of the seed sector won’t be determined by a single scientific breakthrough. It will be determined by whether we can connect all the pieces. We talk constantly about collaboration, but collaboration isn’t just a nice idea anymore. It’s becoming essential infrastructure. Take the work of Texas A&M corn breeder Wenwei Xu. His program aims to combine aflatoxin resistance, improved nutrition, heat and drought tolerance, insect resistance and strong yields into the same hybrid. As Xu points out, farmers won’t adopt a hybrid that excels in one area if it can’t deliver in the field. That’s modern plant breeding in a nutshell. Breeders aren’t solving one problem anymore. They’re solving several at once. Success depends on public research, private investment, field-testing networks and farmers willing to adopt new genetics. The science may begin in a breeding program, but success depends on an ecosystem. That ecosystem also needs people. New National Association for Plant Breeding President Katy Martin Rainey says one of her biggest concerns is attracting the next generation of plant breeders while strengthening collaboration between public programs and private companies. Her message is simple: no one can do this alone. The same challenge is playing out in wheat fields. Farmers have invested heavily in wheat breeding, yet important gaps remain in variety development and testing. Canadian Wheat Research Coalition Chair Jocelyn Velestuk argues that farmers should help lead the next phase, not because they should replace breeders or seed companies, but because someone has to ensure the entire system works together. Leadership isn’t about doing every job. It’s about connecting them. That same principle is behind the new memorandum of underst a nding on Saskatchewan’s Indian Head and Scott research farms, announced in recent days. These facilities are valuable not simply because of their land or buildings, but because they anchor research networks, long-term field trials and scientific expertise that can’t easily be rebuilt once they’re gone. The federal and Saskatchewan governments have agreed to work together to find a temporary way to keep the Indian Head and Scott research farms operating while the federal government continues its formal process of deciding the properties’ long-term future, which could include transferring or selling them. The agreement does not reverse the decision to dispose of the farms, but it aims to ensure they remain productive and continue supporting agriculture in the meantime. At the same time, Agriculture and Agri-Food Canada says it will continue investing in agricultural research through its remaining research centres and by partnering with industry, governments, universities and other organizations across Canada and internationally. Even our upcoming webinar on creating a unified seed transaction platform fits the same pattern. Today’s seed administration is fragmented across ordering, contracting, logistics and invoicing. Every participant may do their own job well, yet the overall experience remains unnecessarily complicated. Amazon didn’t transform retail because it sold products online. It transformed retail because it connected every step of the customer experience into one seamless system. The seed industry doesn’t need to become Amazon. But it does need to embrace the same principle. The common thread running through all these stories is that innovation increasingly happens between organizations, not within them. We need breeders developing better genetics, students entering the profession, and farmers helping shape research priorities. We need governments and industry preserving research capacity, and retailers and seed companies removing friction from how innovation reaches farms. For decades, we’ve viewed public research, private companies, breeding, retail and administration as separate conversations. They’re not. The biggest opportunities now lie in connecting them. The future of seed won’t belong to the organization with the best individual piece, but to the people that learn how to make all the pieces work together.
