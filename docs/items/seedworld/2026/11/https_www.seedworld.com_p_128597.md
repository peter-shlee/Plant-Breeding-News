# Hyperspectral Imaging Could Help Wheat Breeders Select for Resilience

CSIRO researchers are testing whether drone-mounted cameras can identify traits linked to grain filling, water use and yield potential, making them easier to assess across breeding trials. Wheat breeders could gain a faster way to identify promising plants through research using hyperspectral imaging to assess traits that are difficult to measure at scale. CSIRO’s AcceleTraitPlus project is investigating whether the technology can help breeders evaluate photosynthesis, respiration and grain-filling rate across field trials. The aim is to support the development of wheat varieties with higher yield potential, better water use and more reliable performance in difficult seasons. Supported by the Grains Research and Development Corporation (GRDC), the project is testing the approach with a commercial breeding partner to ensure the tools are practical for breeding programs. Looking Beyond Visible Crop Health A wheat plant can appear healthy while using too much water, consuming energy inefficiently or filling its grain too slowly. These differences may be invisible to the human eye, but they can leave signals in the light reflected by leaves. “We use X-rays to see through our bodies to see our bones; similarly, there are things we cannot see that hyperspectral vision technology can see,” said CSIRO senior experimental scientist Aswin Singaram Natarajan. Hyperspectral imaging separates reflected light into hundreds of narrow bands, including wavelengths beyond human vision. Differences in those patterns can provide information about what is happening inside a plant. The researchers are testing whether these spectral signatures can reliably reveal traits that help explain why some wheat varieties perform better under heat, drought and other challenging conditions, according to a press release. Measuring How Wheat Uses Energy Two of the project’s target traits are photosynthesis and respiration. Photosynthesis allows plants to use sunlight and atmospheric carbon dioxide to produce sugars that support growth and grain production. Respiration uses those sugars to maintain cells and support growth, including overnight when photosynthesis stops. Understanding differences in these processes could help breeders assess how effectively wheat plants capture and use energy. However, measuring such traits across hundreds of breeding plots has traditionally been too slow and expensive for routine use. Making Grain-Filling Measurements More Practical Grain-filling rate is another priority and may represent the project’s strongest result so far. The trait helps determine final grain size and contributes to yield. Measuring it manually requires researchers to visit a trial site 10 to 12 times during a season, collect thousands of wheat heads, and dry and weigh the samples. The team is now training a drone-mounted hyperspectral camera to predict grain-filling rate. If successful, the approach could make it easier to assess this trait across large breeding trials and identify promising material for further selection. Building Tools for Commercial Breeding The project forms part of a broader effort to make phenomics — the large-scale measurement of plant traits using sensors and imaging — useful in commercial breeding. GRDC senior manager for genetic technologies (cereals) Jessica Hyles said the investment aimed to help breeders develop wheat varieties offering higher yield potential, better water use and more reliable performance for Australian growers. “Testing these tools with a commercial breeding partner is important to ensure they are practical to use. Ultimately, we want growers to have varieties with higher yield potential, better water use and more reliable field performance.” Dr. Anton Wasson, a project leader in CSIRO’s Agriculture and Food research unit, said substantial investment in plant physiology had resulted in limited adoption by commercial breeding programs. Many promising traits remained difficult to measure affordably at the scale breeders require. By combining detailed field measurements with drone-based imaging, AcceleTraitPlus is working to make those traits more accessible for selection and bring physiological research into routine wheat breeding.
