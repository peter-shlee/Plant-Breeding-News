# Who Really Owns the Seed Customer? Research Challenges Dealer Loyalty Assumptions

Seed dealers spend years building customer relationships. New research suggests much of that loyalty may depend on the brands they sell, raising questions about who holds the power in seed distribution. Imagine spending 20 years building a seed dealership. You know your customers, understand their operations and have helped them make some of their most important agronomic decisions. You’ve earned their trust. Then you lose access to their preferred seed brand. How many of your customers would stay? New research studying nearly 900 large U.S. crop producers offers some surprising insights into how loyalty works across the seed value chain. Producers loyal to a seed brand were approximately 44 percentage points more likely to report loyalty to their dealer than otherwise similar producers who weren’t brand-loyal . The reverse relationship was far weaker: dealer loyalty had no meaningful effect on brand loyalty, according to the research. Put simply, brand loyalty was a much stronger predictor of dealer loyalty than dealer loyalty was of brand loyalty. That imbalance challenges some long-held assumptions about what keeps seed customers coming back. The findings come from research by Chenyu Pan, a master’s student at Purdue (now a Ph.D student at the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign); Trey Malone, Purdue University’s Boehlje Endowed Chair for Managerial Economics in Agribusiness; and Xiurui (Iris) Cui, a postdoctoral fellow at Purdue’s Center for Food and Agricultural Business. Their paper, Asymmetric Loyalty in Vertically Linked B2B Channels: Evidence from the U.S. Seed Market , is currently under review by the International Food and Agribusiness Management Review . Pan says what surprised him most was how “clear the direction was. I expected brand and dealer loyalty to be connected, mainly from brand to dealer, but I expected a weak signal that would need a lot of explaining. Whereas it wasn’t weak.” The survey results reveal how closely those loyalties overlap. Approximately two-thirds of respondents described themselves as loyal to their primary seed dealer, while 60% reported loyalty to a seed brand. About 53% were loyal to both, and 28% to neither. Only about 12% reported loyalty to their dealer without also being loyal to a brand. “That group is the clearest case of loyalty that belongs to the dealer itself,” Pan says. “The dealer relationship is real, but for most farmers it comes bundled with the brand.” The 12% isn’t a precise measure of how much loyalty dealers have earned independently. The survey wasn’t designed to calculate that. Still, it exposes a distinction between having loyal customers and understanding what keeps them loyal. The Loyalty a Dealer Inherits Part of the explanation for why relationships are more dependent on the customer’s attachment to a seed brand than the dealer lies in the cost of switching. “Farmers spend seasons learning how a company’s genetics perform on their ground and tuning planting rates, fertility, and management around them. Switching brands means relearning all of that,” Pan explains. Changing dealers, on the other hand, may be considerably easier. “Farmers commit to the brand, and the dealer who reliably supplies it inherits much of that loyalty,” Pan says. For a seed company, that distinction could change how it evaluates dealer performance and the strength of its distribution network. For an independent dealer, it raises questions about how secure its customer base really is, and whether investing in customer relationships may be ultimately creating more lasting value for their suppliers than for their own businesses. Perhaps the most surprising finding concerns the expertise dealers invest in to differentiate themselves. Pan found that producers who rated their dealers highly for agronomic knowledge, technical advice, service quality and new ideas were more likely to report loyalty to the seed brand. The corresponding relationship with dealer loyalty was weak. Consider what happens when a dealer helps a customer select a hybrid, refine management practices and achieve better results over several seasons. The customer gains confidence in the genetics and becomes increasingly comfortable using them. The dealer has provided the expertise. But the brand may capture much of the resulting loyalty. “The good news for dealers is that brand loyalty then flows back to the dealer who carries the brand. The catch is that it flows to whoever carries the brand,” Pan says. That’s a difficult commercial reality for an independent dealership. Its investment in technical expertise may strengthen demand for products it doesn’t control. For seed companies, the finding puts a different value on dealer training, technical resources and agronomic support. Those investments may contribute to brand equity in ways that sales figures alone don’t reveal. It also raises questions about distribution changes. If customers are primarily committed to a brand, could a seed company move that brand to another dealer without losing many of those customers? The study can’t answer that question because it doesn’t track actual switching behaviour. But it gives seed companies and dealers reason to examine how much customer loyalty might survive a change in distribution. Are Common Assumptions About Retention Correct? The importance of the salesperson relationship also changes depending on what the researchers measure. When the researchers examined dealer loyalty on its own, the salesperson relationship emerged as the strongest dealer-side factor. Location also mattered. Producers who ranked their salesperson relationship among their most important reasons for choosing a dealer were more likely to report loyalty to that dealer. But when brand loyalty was included in the analysis, much of the explanatory strength of those factors diminished. “The standard view in the research is that farmers stay with a dealer mainly because of the relationship. When we look at dealer loyalty alone, the data agrees,” Pan says. “But once we account for brand loyalty, most of those factors lose their explanatory power. They aren’t wrong… The relationship matters, but it often depends on the brand.” For executives evaluating sales teams and dealer networks, customer retention alone may therefore provide an incomplete picture. A strong salesperson and a strong brand can both contribute to a loyal customer, but the research suggests their contributions aren’t equivalent. Interestingly, price, financing, returns and warranties did not significantly distinguish loyal producers from others in the updated analysis. “Competing on price may win a sale, but our data doesn’t show it building loyalty,” Pan says. Producers who placed greater importance on managing costs, production and operations were also less likely to report loyalty to either brands or dealers. That suggests purchasing priorities may be useful to consider when segmenting customers and assessing retention risk, although the study doesn’t establish how those producers would behave when offered competing products. The pattern also holds among younger producers. Older producers were more likely to report loyalty overall, but the connection between brand and dealer loyalty was just as strong among producers aged 35 and younger. “So the next generation appears to build dealer loyalty the same way, through the brand,” Pan says. When the Brand Holds the Cards These loyalty findings have implications for dealer agreements, territory decisions and negotiating power, particularly for independent dealers whose portfolios depend on a relatively small number of suppliers. Pan says dealers should, “First and most importantly, protect access to the brands your customers are committed to. Most dealer loyalty rests on that commitment and this is one of my paper’s biggest findings.” He recommends backing those products with strong agronomic support, then investing in salesperson relationships and local presence, which contribute more directly to loyalty to the dealership. It’s a revealing order of priorities for an industry that has traditionally put personal relationships at the centre of its sales strategy.
