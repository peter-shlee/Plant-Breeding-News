# Aphanomyces Root Rot: A Hidden Threat to Pulse Crop Potential

Aphanomyces root rot can compromise crop health, reduce yield potential and create long-term challenges for pea and lentil growers. Early diagnosis and preventive practices, including fungicide seed treatments, can help suppress the disease and, in some cases, control it. For Canadian pea and lentil growers, aphanomyces root rot can persist beyond a single season. Caused by the soil-borne pathogen, Aphanomyces euteiches , this destructive disease attacks root systems, limiting plants’ abilities to take up water and nutrients when they need them most. Severe infections can cause yield losses of 60-84%* in peas and lentils. Some reports suggest annual losses in Canadian sales and exports could reach $1.5 billion. Proactive management starts with knowing whether fields are infested or at high risk. That kind of impact underscores the need for proactive disease management. Scout Symptoms and Assess Field Conditions Scout low-lying, poorly drained field areas and places where plants appear uneven or stressed. Aphanomyces thrives in warm, moist soils and needs free water to reach and infect roots, making waterlogged areas especially vulnerable. Aphanomyces root rot can be difficult to diagnose early because damage begins underground, compromising crop establishment before symptoms appear above ground. That makes root inspection essential. Carefully dig up symptomatic plants rather than pulling them from the soil. Gently wash the roots and look for soft, water-soaked tissue that turns brown to black as the disease progresses. Compare affected plants with healthy nearby plants, looking for fewer lateral roots and nodules. Because symptoms overlap with other root rots, soil and tissue testing can help confirm the pathogen. As the disease progresses within the roots, the plants may develop nutrient or drought stress and begin to show above-ground symptoms that include yellowing, wilting, stunted growth and even premature plant death. Worse, the threat persists after harvest. Aphanomyces produces resting spores that survive in soil for many years, even during rotations with non-susceptible crops. In-season management options are limited in infested fields, making prevention essential.
