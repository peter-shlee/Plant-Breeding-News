# Japan’s 2026 PVP Law Strengthens Export Controls and Plant Breeders’ Rights

New provisions address unauthorised exports, infringement evidence and compensation, with longer protection periods already in force. Japan knows how valuable a successful plant variety can become. Shine Muscat, the premium green table grape developed by Japan’s National Agriculture and Food Research Organization (NARO), has become an international success, prized for its sweetness, crisp texture and distinctive flavour. Yet its popularity has also exposed a problem that has troubled Japanese breeders for years: varieties developed through substantial investment can become commercially established overseas without their developers receiving the expected returns. The challenges go beyond grapes. Propagating material can cross borders while a variety is still awaiting registration, and once established in another country, it may be multiplied on a considerable scale. Even where plant breeders’ rights (PBR) exist, preventing infringement, obtaining evidence and securing appropriate compensation can prove difficult. The challenge is not simply whether breeders have legal rights, but whether they can exercise them when it matters most. Japan has now responded with another major revision of its Plant Variety Protection and Seed Act, commonly known as the Seed and Seedling Act. Adopted by the Japanese Parliament on 17 July 2026, the amendments introduce earlier protection against unauthorised exports, strengthen enforcement mechanisms and extend the duration of breeders’ rights. While some provisions are already in force, most of the changes will become effective on 1 December 2026. Why Another Reform Was Necessary Japan has been strengthening its variety protection system for several years. In 2020, the country adopted amendments giving breeders greater control over the export of protected varieties to restricted destinations and their cultivation in specified domestic regions. Implemented in stages during 2021 and 2022, these measures were intended to help breeders and agricultural authorities retain control over valuable plant material, particularly varieties developed for premium markets or specialised production areas. However, the earlier reforms did not eliminate every vulnerability. One important gap concerned the period between publication of an application for variety registration and the granting of the right. During this time, which can extend over several years, a variety may already be circulating commercially, creating opportunities for propagating material to leave the country before full protection has been obtained. Minori Hagiwara, Director, International Affairs, Plant Variety Protection Office at Japan’s Ministry of Agriculture, Forestry and Fisheries (MAFF), explains the reasoning behind the latest reform: “Japan has already taken important steps to protect its plant varieties, but practical experience has shown that additional measures are needed. We want breeders to have effective legal tools not only after registration, but also during the period when their varieties may be particularly vulnerable.” The experience of Shine Muscat illustrates the wider difficulty. Registered in Japan in 2006 after many years of breeding, the variety subsequently became widely cultivated elsewhere in Asia, including countries where corresponding PBR protection had not been secured. Not all overseas cultivation necessarily constitutes infringement, since rights are territorial and protection must generally be obtained in each relevant jurisdiction. Nevertheless, the commercial consequences for Japanese breeders and exporters can be substantial. Japan’s reform reflects a broader international discussion about whether existing PBR systems adequately support breeding investment. In Canada, for example, amendments to plant breeders’ rights regulations in 2026 addressed several concerns of horticultural breeders, including farmers’ privilege, the duration of protection for certain crops and administrative procedures. Although the Japanese and Canadian reforms focus on different problems, both demonstrate efforts to adapt established legal frameworks to changing commercial realities. New Powers Before Registration One of the most significant changes in Japan concerns protection during the registration process. Previously, applicants could rely on provisional protection provisions that allowed compensation claims once registration had been granted. However, they did not have a specific mechanism to prevent unauthorised exports while an application was still pending. Minori Hagiwara, Director, International Affairs, Plant Variety Protection Office at Japan’s Ministry of Agriculture, Forestry and Fisheries. Photo: WIPO/Berrod The revised law introduces the possibility of seeking a court injunction against certain exports of propagating material before registration. Once an application has been published, an applicant can issue a written warning to a third party attempting to export the variety. If the export continues and threatens to cause damage that would be difficult to remedy, the applicant may ask a court to prohibit it. The procedure includes safeguards. Courts may require applicants to provide financial security against potential losses suffered by the exporter if the application ultimately fails. Nevertheless, the new mechanism represents an important change in emphasis, allowing breeders to seek intervention before potentially irreversible commercial damage occurs. “Once propagating material has crossed a border, it may be multiplied long before the breeder can respond effectively,” Hagiwara explains. “The possibility of intervening during the registration process can make a significant difference in preventing further unauthorised use.” For breeders of high value horticultural varieties, the practical importance is considerable. A relatively small quantity of propagating material may be sufficient to establish substantial production overseas. Preventing unauthorised exports at an early stage can therefore be more effective than attempting to recover compensation after multiplication has already occurred. Closing the Gap Between Storage and Export A second important change concerns propagating material that is being stored specifically for export. Under the previous framework, breeders could restrict exports of registered varieties to destinations other than any ‘designated destinations’ declared to the MAFF, as destinations where exports were not restricted by the breeder. If the breeder designated ‘no countries’ for a particular variety, then export of that variety is restricted to all countries. However, the act of storing material for such an export was not explicitly covered in the same way. This presented a practical enforcement problem, since the material might already be prepared for shipment before the breeder could intervene. The amended law extends protection to storage clearly intended for export to a restricted destination. According to MAFF’s explanatory guidance , examples include propagating material held in an airport’s bonded area or in a domestic warehouse with labels showing that it is destined for overseas shipment. Such circumstances can provide objective evidence that the material is being stored for export. This is not a general prohibition on international trade in propagating material. The new provision strengthens the enforceability of existing restrictions on particular varieties and export destinations. It allows breeders to act before the material leaves Japanese territory, when domestic enforcement mechanisms are still available. The distinction between the two new export related provisions is important. One protects applicants during the period before registration, while the other extends the scope of protection for registered varieties to material stored for restricted exports. Both are intended to close gaps that previously complicated enforcement. Making Infringement Easier to Prove Preventing unauthorised exports is only one aspect of effective plant variety protection. Breeders must also be able to demonstrate infringement and obtain meaningful remedies when their rights are violated. A notable amendment introduces a rebuttable legal presumption concerning the identity of propagating material sold under the denomination of a registered variety. Where seed or other propagating material is marketed using that denomination, it will be presumed to belong to that variety for the purposes of civil enforcement proceedings. Previously, establishing varietal identity could require the rights holder to obtain samples and commission growing trials or DNA analysis. Such investigations can be costly and time consuming, particularly where material is difficult to access. The new presumption can reduce that evidentiary burden, although the opposing party may challenge it. The breeder must still establish that an unauthorised act has occurred. Shine Muscat grapes growing in a NARO vineyard in Japan. The variety has become a prominent example in discussions about protecting Japanese plant breeding innovations, particularly against unauthorised exports of propagating material. Japan’s 2026 PVP law revisions aim to strengthen that protection. Photo: NARO. Another important amendment concerns the calculation of damages. Under the revised provisions, compensation may include a royalty equivalent amount for infringing quantities exceeding the breeder’s own capacity to produce or sell the material. Courts may also take the infringement itself into account when determining an appropriate hypothetical licence fee, potentially resulting in a higher amount than an ordinary negotiated royalty. These changes recognise that the economic impact of infringement cannot always be measured solely by the sales the breeder could have made personally. Unauthorised multiplication can undermine licensing opportunities, commercial arrangements and the market value of exclusive rights. “For plant breeders’ rights to have real value, breeders need realistic ways to establish infringement and obtain appropriate remedies,” says Hagiwara. “Improving the rules concerning evidence and damages is therefore an important part of making the protection system more effective in practice.” Several additional changes complement these measures. A new priority examination mechanism will allow certain applications to be examined more quickly, including cases involving commercial use of an applicant’s variety by another party. Courts will also be able to invite third party opinions on relevant legal questions in infringement proceedings as increasingly plant variety protection (PVP) court decisions have impact on the entire industry other than just the parties themselves. Additionally, provisions concerning the lending of propagating material extend protection to particular distribution arrangements. The legislation additionally clarifies that authorised sales of propagating material abroad do not exhaust the breeder’s rights in Japan. This confirms the existing requirement for appropriate authorisation when protected material is imported into Japan. Longer Protection for Registered Varieties The reform also extends the duration of plant breeders’ rights by ten years. Protection now lasts 35 years for most plants and 40 years for perennial plants, compared with the previous periods of 25 and 30 years respectively. The Japanese government has justified the extension partly by pointing to the long-term commercial value of certain varieties, particularly those developed by public breeding institutions and used to support distinctive regional agricultural production. Longer protection can provide additional time to recover breeding and commercialisation investments. Unlike most other amendments, the extension took effect on 24 July 2026. It applies to registrations that remained valid on that date but does not revive rights that had already expired. Beyond Japan’s Borders The PVP amendments form part of a broader effort to strengthen Japanese plant breeding. In July 2026, Japan also adopted complementary legislation promoting the development of important varieties and the production of their propagating material. This legislation aims to support breeding that responds to challenges such as climate change, agricultural productivity and stable food supplies. Internationally, Japan is not alone in reassessing the effectiveness of plant variety protection. China has also strengthened its PVP framework, with the first operational implementation of its essentially derived variety system beginning in July 2026 for 10 crops. While China’s focus on essentially derived varieties differs from Japan’s emphasis on export controls and enforcement, both countries are adapting their frameworks to address issues arising from modern plant breeding and commercialisation. These developments are also relevant in Europe, where the ongoing evaluation of the Community Plant Variety Rights system , including questions surrounding provisional protection and farm-saved seed. Although the Japanese and European systems operate in different legal and agricultural environments, Japan’s amendments provide an interesting example of how practical enforcement problems can drive legislative change. Hagiwara considers effective protection essential for continued innovation. “Developing new varieties requires sustained investment, specialist knowledge and time,” she says. “By strengthening the protection available to breeders, we want to encourage continued innovation and support the development of varieties that can contribute to the future of agriculture.” Japan’s latest reform underlines a principle that extends well beyond its borders: granting plant breeders’ rights is an important starting point, but those rights must also be enforceable. The new legislation seeks to close the gap between protection on paper and protection that works in practice. What Has Changed?
