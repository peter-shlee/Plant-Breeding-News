# The Missing Link In Canada’s Breeding Future | On The Brink: Season 3, Episode 11

https://youtu.be/L3ZhMMdfev8 What Seed Tests Could Tell Plant Breeders Sarah Foster says seed testing data could contribute more to Canada’s breeding future if analysts, growers and breeders had a stronger way to share what they learn. Seed testing can help growers understand disease concerns, germination problems and unusually high dormancy. Sarah Foster wants a clearer route for that information to reach the people developing Canada’s next crop varieties. Speaking from the purity lab at 20/20 Seed Labs in Nisku, Alberta, Foster says analysts have a perspective that belongs in the discussion about the future of plant breeding. Their work connects them with growers and the seed quality challenges those growers face. “We can give that information very easily back to a grower, but I don’t know that necessarily breeders are in that loop.” What seed testing can reveal Foster starts with the farmer’s knowledge. Nobody knows their fields and how varieties perform better than the people growing them, she says. Seed analysts provide a support network that can help investigate concerns. If disease emerges in a region, labs can report what they are finding. If samples of a particular crop show high dormancy, analysts can investigate whether the issue appears across a region or is isolated to a smaller area. Those findings give growers information they can use. Foster sees an opportunity for that knowledge to contribute more broadly to the breeding conversation as well. Connecting seed testing data with plant breeding The challenge, in Foster’s view, is that information is divided among different areas of testing. A germination concern may be considered separately from a disease concern, and the results may not feed into a wider discussion with breeders. “All of that information that we’re capturing is actually kind of siloed into different areas.” She wants seed analysts to work more closely with customers to understand what is happening and what can be done. She also believes some quality assessments could go into greater depth. Her proposal centres on making better use of observations already being collected. A stronger connection between growers, analysts and breeders could help bring recurring seed quality concerns into discussions about future research and variety development. Why grower input matters alongside funding Foster points to Australia as a model that resonates with her, particularly the combination of financial support and grower information feeding into research. She sees value in a system where farmers help inform the work they support. Australia’s Grains Research and Development Corporation provides relevant context. The Australian government’s 2025–26 portfolio budget statements describe GRDC as primarily funded through a partnership between grain growers and government. GRDC’s own account of its National Grower Network describes how local issues and grower priorities help shape research, development and extension investments. For Foster, the appeal is the connection between support for research and information from the people growing crops. She wants Canada’s conversation to make room for the knowledge farmers and seed analysts can contribute. Seed analysts need a place in the conversation Foster wants the industry to take a more business-focused approach to its future and bring stakeholders into the same discussion. For seed analysts, that means contributing their understanding of seed quality and the questions growers bring to the lab. “We absolutely need to be part of the conversation when it comes to what’s really happening in the field.
