# Matching Crop Choice to Tree Age Could Protect Agroforestry Yields

Winter cereals maintained yields alongside poplar rows, while spring crops performed best early in the tree rotation, a German study finds. Choosing winter or spring crops according to the age of neighbouring trees could help farmers maintain crop yields while gaining the additional benefits of agroforestry. Research led by the University of Göttingen found that winter crops grown alongside rows of fast-growing poplars produced yields similar to those in treeless fields across the tree-growth stages examined. Spring crops, however, experienced yield reductions as the trees matured and cast more shade. The findings, published in Agronomy for Sustainable Development , suggest that adjusting crop rotations to tree growth could help farmers balance grain and wood production. Managing Competition Between Trees and Crops Growing crops such as wheat, barley and rye between rows of trees is known as alley cropping. The system can improve soil quality, reduce wind erosion, support biodiversity and increase carbon capture. Trees can also compete with crops for water, sunlight and nutrients. Whether they help or hinder crop production depends partly on their size and the crop’s distance from the tree rows. To investigate these interactions, researchers measured crop yields at different distances from poplar rows over six years at four sites in Germany, according to a press release. The trees were managed as short-rotation coppice, in which they are cut back to near ground level every three to nine years and allowed to regrow. Older Trees Reduced Spring Crop Yields When trees were five years old or older, spring crop yields in the agroforestry systems were between 10% and 35% lower than those in treeless fields. The largest reductions occurred within four metres of the tree rows, primarily because shade limited the light available to crops. Winter crops showed a different response. Their yields remained similar to those in treeless fields across the nine years of tree growth examined, indicating greater flexibility for including them throughout the tree rotation. Timing Crop Rotations to Tree Harvests The results point to a practical management approach: concentrate spring crops in the first three years after trees are harvested, when regrowth creates less shade, and use winter crops throughout the rotation. “Our study shows that farmers can counteract the negative effects that the shade from trees has on crops by growing spring crops only during the first three years following the harvest of trees, whereas winter crops can be grown throughout the entire rotation because they are less affected by tree shade,” said Dr Sarah Choe, who conducted the research as part of her PhD. The findings also help explain why trees can reduce yields nearby while benefiting crops farther away. “The benefit of trees is that they can protect against high wind and this reduces moisture loss from the crop growing a bit further away from the trees. This can, in fact, sometimes make up for the losses in harvest close to the tree rows,” said Professor Edzo Veldkamp, who led the research at Göttingen’s Faculty of Forest Sciences and Forest Ecology. Combining Grain and Wood Production The researchers also found that annual wood production was three times higher after the second tree harvest than during the earlier harvest periods. “We were also able to show that there was increased wood production from the poplar trees after the second tree harvest. This means that with the right choice of winter or spring crops, alley cropping agroforestry outperforms open farmland in terms of productivity,” Choe said. For farmers considering alley cropping, the study highlights the importance of planning crop and tree rotations together. Matching crop choice to changing shade conditions could help protect cereal yields while adding wood production and environmental benefits.
