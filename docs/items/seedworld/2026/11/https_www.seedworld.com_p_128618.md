# Stacy Kuenzi Leads Western Seed Association Into the Future

After 28 years at Mountain View Seeds, Stacy Kuenzi knows what keeps people in the seed business. As Western Seed Association’s new president, she’s thinking about how to get more of them through the door. When Stacy Kuenzi was offered a job selling grass seed nearly 28 years ago, she had one thought: “Really? Who sells grass seed?” Raised on an Iowa farm, Kuenzi had just finished college and gotten married when she agreed to help out for a couple of weeks at Oregon’s Pratum Co-op. Soon after, she was invited to join its fledgling subsidiary, Mountain View Seeds. “I knew nothing about grass seed. I knew nothing about anything in this industry,” she recalls. She took the job anyway. Starting as one of three employees, Kuenzi learned the business from the ground up, first operations, then sales. Today, she’s Mountain View’s vice president of sales, leading a team serving turf, forage and cover crop markets. She’s also the new president of the Western Seed Association (WSA) as of the organization’s November convention in Kansas City. “Where business gets done” Western Seed Association President Stacy Kuenzi Founded in 1899, ‘the Western’ remains one of North America’s most important seed trading gatherings, bringing together producers, distributors, buyers and suppliers for the critical meetings that drive the seed business. “People truly do business at the Western. You get that handshake,” Kuenzi says. “You have your own time set aside with potential partners. That in-person connection is what makes it possible to get a lot of work done.” Those relationships matter especially in difficult times. In grass seed, oversupply and price volatility have squeezed successive parts of the supply chain. Distributors were caught holding expensive inventory, seed companies faced their own pricing challenges and now growers are absorbing much of the pressure. Add tariffs, rising labour costs, fuel and other inputs, and profitability is a concern across the industry. “It seems like every year somebody new takes the hit,” she says. “Things are finally trending the right direction now. I hope this year everyone can be successful and prosperous. We need that as an industry.” Who’s Coming Next? As WSA president, Kuenzi wants to draw attention to a challenge shared across Western’s membership: attracting the next generation of seed professionals before decades of experience walk out the door. “It’s so difficult to get people to come into this industry.” Ag students may think about agronomy, but rarely seed sales or operations, she says. Scholarships, like the ones offered by WSA, can introduce young people to seed careers, but Kuenzi sees opportunities to do more through social media, facility tours and internships. At Mountain View, partnerships with Oregon State University bring students through the company’s facilities, while internships provide hands-on experience. She’d like to see more opportunities like those across the industry. “There’s so much industry knowledge that has left since I’ve been in this,” she says. “We need to get people in to gain that knowledge before these people retire.” She also values Western’s Seedsman of the Year award as another opportunity to elevate emerging talent, build professional networks and encourage long-term careers. Kuenzi remembers how intimidating her first Western convention felt, particularly as one of relatively few women attending. Experienced colleagues introduced her to people and helped her find her place. Nearly three decades later, she’s leading the same association. She hopes more young people get the opportunity she received and, like her, discover a career in seed they never knew existed.
